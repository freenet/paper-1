\section{How Data Is Found}
\label{sec:routing}

A request for contract $\contract$ originates at some peer and must reach a peer that hosts $\contract$'s state. Peer and contract locations and the ring metric are as defined in \cref{sec:primitives}. This section describes the ring topology, the rule that constructs it and the bootstrap by which a new peer joins, how requests are forwarded and how the five operations use that forwarding, and the performance model that selects each hop. It closes with a 24-hour measurement of routing path lengths on the live network.

\subsection{Why Small-World Routing}
\label{sec:smallworld}

The routing strategy derives from Kleinberg's small-world result \citep{kleinberg2000small}. Consider $N$ peers on a ring (or a lattice of any fixed dimension), each with a constant number of \emph{short-range} links to immediate neighbors and a small number of \emph{long-range} links sampled with probability proportional to $1/d$ in ring distance $d$. Kleinberg proved that a node with knowledge limited to its neighbors can greedily route to any target in $O(\log^2 N)$ hops. The result requires neither global topology knowledge nor flooding, provided the link distribution remains approximately $1/d$.

The $1/d$ distribution is what makes the bound work. With shorter long-range links, a hop does not reduce the remaining distance much; with longer ones, hops overshoot the target. With $1/d$, at every scale a peer has a constant probability of a link that halves the remaining distance, so each halving takes $O(\log N)$ expected steps and $O(\log N)$ halvings reach the target.

Kleinberg's analysis assumes the $1/d$ distribution. Symphony \citep{manku2003symphony} sampled long-range links from a harmonic distribution at join time and retained them. Freenet instead adjusts each peer's neighborhood continuously. A peer directs new \textsf{CONNECT} requests toward under-represented regions of its current connection distribution (\emph{gap-targeting}) and prefers incoming candidates that close large gaps (\emph{Kleinberg-style scoring}). These mechanisms maintain the distribution under churn.

\subsection{Topology Construction}

The \textsf{CONNECT} operation constructs the small-world topology. A joiner chooses a \emph{desired location}, routes a \textsf{CONNECT} message toward it, and receives acceptance decisions along the route and at its terminus. Target selection and acceptance both shape the topology toward Kleinberg's $1/d$ distribution \citep{kleinberg2000small}.

\input{figures/ring-gap.tex}

\paragraph{Desired location.} A peer with only a few connections targets its own location, with random jitter after repeated acceptance failures so that later attempts probe different parts of the ring. Because joiners' locations differ, their early targets differ too, and bootstrap traffic spreads across the ring. A peer with more connections instead computes a \emph{gap target}: it measures the ring distance from itself to each existing neighbor, finds the largest gap in that distribution in log-distance space, and targets the gap's midpoint. The locations under-represented by its current connections are thus exactly the ones its next \textsf{CONNECT} targets, and a peer with mostly local neighbors, whose largest gap is at long range, next connects to a distant peer. This is how each peer pulls its own connection distribution toward $1/d$.

\paragraph{Acceptance.} A peer that receives a \textsf{CONNECT} forwards it to a neighbor no farther from the target than itself, if it has one. A peer within a small band around the target may also accept the joiner, with a probability that grows with proximity, while still forwarding; this gives the joiner more than one nearby connection per request.

At the terminus, the decision depends on the receiver's connection count. A peer with very few connections accepts unconditionally, so that bootstrap can proceed. Below its minimum it scores the candidate by how well the candidate's location fills gaps among its existing neighbors, accepting more selectively as it fills. At or above the minimum it admits only the best-scoring candidate among recent arrivals, and above the maximum it rejects. If the terminus rejects, the \textsf{CONNECT} may be forwarded a few hops away from the target, so that a joiner whose target lies behind a saturated peer can still be accepted by a nearby peer with capacity.

\input{figures/connect-path.tex}

\paragraph{Nearest-neighbor lattice.} Kleinberg's bound also assumes a short-range base lattice. Each peer therefore seeks and keeps its nearest successor and predecessor on the ring. Maintenance \textsf{CONNECT}s routed toward the peer's own location discover closer candidates on either side; a closer candidate is admitted even when the peer is at capacity, and pruning never discards the closest edge on either side. The remaining connections form the long-range distribution described above.

\paragraph{Joining and NAT traversal.} Most peers are behind consumer NAT and have no stable inbound address. Two peers connect by hole-punching: both send hello packets toward each other's externally observed addresses at the same time \citep{ford2005peer}. The \emph{introducer}, which reports each side's observed address to the other, is whichever peer routed the \textsf{CONNECT} between them. For a new peer this is a gateway, a peer with a public address; for a peer that already has a connection, it is one of its neighbors. Gateways are therefore needed only for the first connection, and the network continues to form connections while every gateway is offline.

\subsection{Forwarding}

A request from peer $p$ to target location $t$ proceeds as follows. Let $\Gamma(p)$ be $p$'s current set of neighbors, excluding peers the request has already visited. A peer without enough routing history orders $\Gamma(p)$ by distance to $t$, placing first the neighbors it has not yet routed through so that it gathers evidence about each of them. Once it has that history, it takes a fixed number of the neighbors closest to $t$ as candidates and selects among them by the predicted cost of \cref{sec:adaptive}. That number is close to a peer's minimum connection count, so distance acts mainly through the cost model rather than as a filter. Kleinberg's $O(\log^2 N)$ bound (\cref{sec:smallworld}) is proved for strict greedy routing, in which every hop moves closer to the target; we do not claim it for this cost-ranked form.

Each request carries a \emph{hops-to-live} (HTL) counter, deployed maximum 10, that decrements on each forward: when it reaches zero, the current peer stops forwarding and answers from local state or reports a miss. A peer that is near $t$ but does not host the contract forwards to its next-best candidate, and visited peers are never revisited.

\subsection{Lifecycles}

The five operations of \cref{sec:primitives} are realized as transactions, each driven to completion by its own task in the local core, with timeouts, retries, and parent/child composition.

\paragraph{\textsf{CONNECT}.} As above. Each successful \textsf{CONNECT} establishes one neighbor relationship. \textsf{CONNECT}s are issued by a maintenance loop, not as a side effect of ordinary request traffic.

\paragraph{\textsf{PUT}.} A \textsf{PUT} carries a contract $\contract$ and an initial state $\sigma_0$. The originator validates and stores the state, computes its summary, and routes a probe toward $\ell(\contract)$; intermediate peers that do not host the contract forward the probe without storing anything. If the probe reaches a peer that already hosts the contract, that peer returns its summary, and the originator sends it the corresponding delta, which then propagates through the co-host mesh like any update. Otherwise the operation falls back to sending the full state, which the peers along the route validate and host. A \textsf{PUT} ends when its route has descended toward $\ell(\contract)$ and no remaining candidate is closer.

\paragraph{\textsf{GET}.} A \textsf{GET} carries a key $k$ and is routed toward the corresponding location. Any peer on the path that hosts the contract may answer with its current state and parameters, and the response returns along the request path. A route that ends without finding a host does not immediately report a miss: the last peer consults its neighbors' host advertisements (\cref{sec:updates}) and forwards to a neighbor that hosts the contract, if one exists. Forwarding reaches the right part of the ring, and the advertisements supply the final step. A retried \textsf{GET} avoids first hops that already reported the contract missing.

The requester can verify that the served code and parameters hash to $k$, but not the state: the key is derived from code and parameters, and state is mutable. This differs from immutable content-addressed stores such as IPFS, where the value returned for $k$ itself hashes to $k$. State is trusted only to the extent that the contract's validity predicate accepts it (\cref{sec:trust}).

\paragraph{\textsf{UPDATE}.} An \textsf{UPDATE} carries a key $k$ and a candidate state $u \in \statespace_\contract$ and is routed toward $\ell(\contract)$. Each peer on the path that hosts the contract merges $u$ into its copy, subject to the validity check, and sends the change to its co-hosts (\cref{sec:updates}).

\paragraph{\textsf{SUBSCRIBE}.} A \textsf{SUBSCRIBE} is routed toward $\ell(\contract)$ and ends like a \textsf{GET}; the peers along the path record the subscription and host the contract, forming the leased demand path of \cref{sec:updates}.

\subsection{Adaptive Routing}
\label{sec:adaptive}

Ring distance alone does not identify the fastest path because neighbors may differ by an order of magnitude or more in bandwidth, RTT, and uptime. The router therefore selects the next hop by predicted total cost.

The local peer maintains a history of routing events of the form $(q,d,c,\tau,b,f)$, where $q$ is the selected neighbor, $d$ is its ring distance to the request's target, $c$ is the contract's location, $\tau$ is the observed time-to-first-byte, $b$ is the observed transfer rate, and $f$ is a failure indicator. A request that ends in a miss or a timeout counts as a failure of the route that served it, so the failure signal covers the neighbor's onward path as well as the neighbor itself. From this history the router predicts three outcomes for each candidate:
\[
  \widehat{\tau}_q(d), \quad \widehat{p}_q(d), \quad \widehat{b}_q(d),
\]
the expected latency, failure probability, and throughput. Failure is modeled as a probability, and latency and throughput on a logarithmic scale. Each prediction has two parts: a distance curve $g(d)$ shared by all neighbors, and a hierarchy of corrections specific to the neighbor and the part of the ring the contract lies in.

\paragraph{Distance prior.} The shared curve $g$ is fit by \emph{isotonic regression} \citep{barlow1972statistical, deleeuw2009isotone}, the standard non-parametric estimator for a monotone function. The choice of isotonic regression encodes a structural prior we are willing to commit to (latency and failure are non-decreasing in distance-to-target, on average) without committing to a parametric family for the shape of the curve. The pool-adjacent-violators fit is shrunk toward the pooled mean, so that a block supported by few events cannot dominate it, and then re-fit to restore monotonicity.

\input{figures/adaptive-routing.tex}

\paragraph{Hierarchical corrections.} Neighbors differ from the shared curve in ways that persist: one has a slow uplink, another selectively drops requests for one part of the key space. The router models the residual $r = y - g(d)$ of each observed outcome $y$ as a sum of effects at three levels: a network-wide offset, a per-neighbor offset, and a per-neighbor offset for each of several fixed arcs of the ring, assigned by contract location. A prediction descends this hierarchy: at each level the estimate moves from its parent's value toward the level's own mean by the fraction $P/(P+V)$, where $P$ is the variance of effects at that level and $V$ the sampling variance of the level's own mean. Both variances are estimated from the data, in the manner of parametric empirical Bayes \citep{morris1983eb}. A neighbor observed twice therefore moves its prediction little, while one observed a thousand times is predicted mostly from its own record.

\paragraph{Forgetting.} Each level discounts older events exponentially. The rate of discounting is selected online: the router keeps one hierarchy for each of several rates and predicts with whichever has the lowest recent loss on outcomes it forecast before observing them. Onward-path quality changes rapidly under churn because it depends on peers beyond the immediate link, and this lets the model track it without fixing in advance how quickly it changes.

\paragraph{Contract effects.} A failure on a contract that no peer can serve is not evidence about the neighbor that was asked. If learned as such, it would raise that neighbor's predicted failure rate for every other contract. The failure model therefore adds a per-contract effect, estimated from the outcomes of \emph{other} neighbors on the same contract, and subtracts it from what the neighbor-level terms learn. A neighbor that fails on every contract is still charged with its own failures, because a neighbor's own outcomes never contribute to the contract effect subtracted from its record.

\paragraph{Selection.} Given a target at distance $d_t$ from candidate $q$, the router selects the candidate that minimizes the expected cost
\[
  \widehat{\tau}_q(d_t) + \bar{s}/\widehat{b}_q(d_t) + 3\,\widehat{\tau}_q(d_t)\,\widehat{p}_q(d_t),
\]
where $\bar{s}$ is the mean transfer size, so a failure is charged as roughly three further response times. Timing is observed only on successful transfers, and a candidate without a timing estimate is ranked by $3\,\widehat{p}_q(d_t)$ alone. The timing predictions account for the uncertainty in each neighbor's own estimate, so a neighbor with little history is predicted to be slower than an established one with the same typical performance. While an outcome's hierarchical estimate lacks data, the router uses a simpler estimator for it.

Because the model consumes end-to-end routing outcomes, its predictions combine the neighbor's link quality (RTT, bandwidth, and reliability) with the quality of onward paths through that neighbor.

\textsf{CONNECT} does not use this model. It forwards only to neighbors no farther from the target than the current peer, ranked by a separate estimator of acceptance, so that topology-construction traffic keeps moving toward its target.

\subsection{A 24-Hour Snapshot of Routing on the Deployed Network}
\label{sec:routing-measurement}

\input{figures/connection-distance-histogram.tex}

This subsection records a single observation of routing on the deployed network, as a consistency check rather than a scaling study. It predates the estimator described above: requests were then ranked by an earlier model that blended the isotonic distance curve with a k-nearest-neighbor predictor \citep{renegade}. All peers ran with telemetry enabled, so every hop was observed. The 24-hour window ends 2026-05-24; 443 peers were active in its last 10 minutes and 604 distinct peers were seen over the whole window, the difference being churn. A snapshot of connection distances taken the following day (\cref{fig:connection-distance}) shows the qualitative small-world shape the topology rule is designed to produce. It is more heavy-tailed than a pure $1/d$ law, which is not in itself better: Kleinberg's bound depends on the tail exponent, and relating the tail exponent to hop count requires the measurements across network sizes discussed below.

For each \textsf{GET} and \textsf{PUT} transaction we compute a \emph{path length}: the number of distinct peers seen emitting the corresponding \textsf{*\_request} event for that transaction id, which is the number of peers visited (hops $+ 1$). Statistics over operations whose telemetry reached completion in the window:\footnote{In this May 2026 snapshot, \textsf{UPDATE} (median path length 2, $n = 386$) and \textsf{SUBSCRIBE} (median 1, $n = 5{,}359$) propagated primarily through pre-existing subscription paths rather than fresh routes, so their path lengths reflected the distance from the subscriber to the nearest hosting peer rather than routing-layer hop counts. They are excluded from the table here.}

The measurement has two further limitations. First, it covers one observation at one network size. At $N \approx 443$, $\log_2 N \approx 8.8$; the observed median path length of 7 peers (6 hops) for \textsf{GET} is consistent with logarithmic-diameter routing, but a single data point at a single $N$ cannot distinguish $O(\log N)$ from $O(\sqrt{N})$, $O(N^{0.4})$, or a constant. Second, the table does not compare against any baseline, so it does not isolate the contribution of adaptive routing from distance-ordered forwarding. Both require measurements across orders of magnitude in $N$, which remain open (\cref{sec:status}).
