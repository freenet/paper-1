\section{Implementation Status and Open Problems}
\label{sec:status}

This section describes the reference implementation, reports the status of each major mechanism, identifies the applications the platform suits and those it does not, and lists unresolved questions.

\subsection{Implementation}

The Freenet Core is an asynchronous runtime written in Rust. Contracts and delegates execute in Wasmtime \citep{wasmtime} under time and memory limits, so that a runaway or malicious module cannot exhaust the host, and contract state is persisted locally. Each operation runs as its own asynchronous task, so a peer's throughput is not limited to what a single event loop can sustain.

\paragraph{Deterministic simulation testing.} A peer-to-peer system is hard to test by ordinary means: many bugs in routing, congestion, or convergence appear only in specific multi-peer interleavings that are difficult to reproduce. The Core ships with a deterministic simulation harness built on Turmoil \citep{turmoil}: virtual time, a seeded global RNG, an in-memory socket implementation, and fault injection. Tests run the entire network in a single deterministic process and replay bit-for-bit from a seed. Most non-trivial routing, subscription, and contract-execution bugs we have found were first reproduced in simulation.

Congestion control is the least mature component. The deployed default is a fixed-rate token bucket; LEDBAT++ and BBR variants exist as alternatives, but neither has yet been adapted satisfactorily to peer-to-peer links of widely varying capacity.

\subsection{Application Fit}

Contract-defined merge, summary/delta synchronization, small-world routing, and delegates suit applications whose conflicts can be resolved by application-defined merge, such as group chat, collaborative documents, social feeds, reputation systems, asynchronous inboxes, and identity directories. A chat application \citep{river} and an anonymous identity scheme \citep{ghostkey} are deployed on the live network. Three classes of application fit less well.

\paragraph{Strictly linearizable transactions.} A defense against double-spending fungible tokens requires that two attempts to spend the same balance cannot both succeed. The merge model cannot express ``one of these updates is rejected unconditionally''; merge is total. Applications that need a strong-consistency primitive must either contain the inconsistency inside a contract whose validity predicate detects and rejects the second update (which forces the conflict into application code, with the loser's state restored on detection) or layer an auxiliary consensus mechanism on top of the platform.

The limitation extends beyond currency. A reputation system that only \emph{accumulates} endorsements is monotone and fits the merge model. Revoking a credential, slashing a stake, or reducing a balance introduces the same requirement to reject a conflicting second update. Applications that otherwise fit the model may therefore require an additional consistency mechanism for non-monotone operations.

\paragraph{Abuse-resistant open posting.} A purely open contract is simple to write as a set under union and equally simple to flood. Practical contracts in this class restrict writes to signed records by approved keys, charge a proof-of-work fee per update, or require credentials issued by a reputation contract. The per-neighbor limits of \cref{sec:trust} cap what one attacker can push through one peer, but they do not stop a distributed flood from many cheap identities or low-rate abuse that stays within per-peer limits, and the platform has no other spam defense.

\paragraph{Durable storage of cold state.} Contract state persists only while some peer retains it, and eviction (\cref{sec:updates}) removes the least-demanded contracts first: those with no readers or subscribers. The platform does not recruit a replacement when a host evicts a contract, so a contract nobody reads or subscribes to can disappear from every host, recoverable only by a new \textsf{PUT} from a party that still holds its state. The platform therefore provides no durability guarantee for cold state, and dormant collaborative documents, unpolled inboxes, and identity records of inactive users may stay cold for long periods.

\subsection{Open Problems}

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.4em, itemsep=2pt]
  \item \textbf{Scaling characterization.} The measurement in \cref{sec:routing-measurement} is consistent with logarithmic-diameter routing at one network size. Characterizing scaling requires measurements across orders of magnitude in $N$, which the current deployment cannot provide and the simulation harness does not yet produce. The routing estimator's structure was likewise selected in simulation, and the contribution of its per-contract failure term has not yet been established on production traffic.
  \item \textbf{Enforcing the merge laws.} Containment and conformance testing (\cref{sec:conformance}) detect some violations, but neither removes a faulty contract from the network. Running conformance testing across the network, propagating its evidence so that other peers can re-verify it, and ceasing to host contracts with confirmed violations remain open. Coverage is also incomplete. A contract that rejects the merge of two individually valid states is reported as inconclusive, although the rejection means the replicas cannot converge, and very large states are not tested. Testing cannot constrain a malicious author, but it gives honest authors a repeatable check.
  \item \textbf{Congestion control} suited to heterogeneous peer-to-peer links.
  \item \textbf{Sybil-resistant identity.} Identity and reputation contracts are routed through the location layer they are meant to secure (\cref{sec:trust}). Breaking this circularity requires ring separation among replicas of safety-critical contracts, demonstrable diversity near their locations, or out-of-band trust anchors. Spreading peers that share an address prefix without restoring the freedom to choose locations is also open.
  \item \textbf{Incentive and abuse layer.} The network needs a way to resist coordinated denial of service without centralized admission control or excessive barriers to joining.
  \item \textbf{Durability of cold state.} Maintaining a minimum replica count independently of demand, through active repair, incentivized pinning, or archival contracts, without creating an unbounded free-storage abuse surface.
\end{enumerate}
